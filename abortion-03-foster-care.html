The cliff
In a Milwaukee County study, children with 1 caseworker reached a permanent home 74.5% of the time. Children with 2 caseworkers: 17.5%. Children with 6 or more caseworkers: 0.1%. The system disrupts itself.

From the caseworker's side
What one caseworker is responsible for

Now flip the camera around. The same churn that costs each child their relationships traces back to a single overworked person on the other side. One caseworker. 22 active cases. Every case is a child plus a constellation of homes, courts, schools, and therapists. The math doesn't work.

The arithmetic of failure
A caseworker earning $53,940 is responsible for the legal, emotional, educational, medical, and family-relationship coordination of roughly 22 children. That's $2,452 of caseworker time per child per year — the same a family might spend on a phone plan or a cheap used car. Each child requires monthly home visits, court hearings every 6 months, school coordination, therapist updates, foster-parent support, and birth-family contact plans. Quality casework at this caseload is mathematically impossible. Burnout is the predictable result, and turnover is the predictable consequence.

Sources: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Social Workers (May 2024) — median annual wage $53,940 for child, family, and school social workers; BLS via ABA Edu — entry level $37,900 (10th percentile), top 10% above $85,590; Child Welfare League of America Standards — recommended caseload 12–15; EPIC at Ohio State — actual averages 24–31. The home-level count (~88) assumes 4 placements/relationships per child (birth family, current foster home, 1–2 potential permanent homes); courts, schools, therapists, attorneys, and biological siblings add further layers not pictured.

The trauma the system creates

Lifetime PTSD rates · Casey National Alumni Study, n=1,087
Foster care alumni experience PTSD at rates that exceed combat veterans

A child in foster care experiences two compounding traumas: the original harm that brought them into the system, and the disruption of being removed from their family and moved between homes. The result is measurable in adulthood. Roughly 1 in 5 foster care alumni meets clinical criteria for PTSD — nearly five times the general-population rate, and higher than U.S. veterans of Iraq, Afghanistan, or Vietnam.

A note on what this means
The foster system is built to remove children from harm. By that measure it succeeds, in the immediate term. But the data shows that removal itself is traumatizing, and the system's pattern of placement instability deepens rather than treats the trauma it inherits. Veterans of combat receive structured trauma care through the VA. Foster alumni typically receive no comparable system of post-care mental health support — even though they carry similar or greater PTSD burdens, recover at lower rates, and entered through no choice of their own.

Adoption from foster care: the incentives

Federal & state support for adoptive families · Title IV-E and beyond
For foster-care adoption, the government pays you

The foster care system funds the path to permanence in a way most readers don't know exists. When a foster child is adopted, federal and state programs continue paying the adoptive family monthly — often for the entire childhood of the child. Private and international adoption can cost $25–65K upfront with no recurring support. Foster-care adoption is essentially the opposite: low cost to begin, ongoing financial support throughout the child's upbringing.

What adoption assistance typically includes
  • Monthly maintenance payments — negotiated per child, capped at the state's foster care rate. Cannot exceed what the state would have paid a foster family.
  • Medicaid coverage for the child — transfers across state lines for federally-eligible (Title IV-E) cases.
  • One-time non-recurring expenses — up to ~$1,500 per child for legal fees, court costs, agency fees, and travel.
  • Federal adoption tax credit — $16,810 per child in 2024, claimable against federal income tax.
  • Continued post-adoption services in many states — counseling, respite care, support groups, training.
  • State employee benefits in some jurisdictions — additional adoption assistance payments for state employees.
The cost flip
Private domestic adoption costs $25,000–$65,000 upfront with no recurring support. Foster-care adoption costs ~$0–$2,500 upfront and pays the family roughly $5,000–$10,000 per year for the child's entire upbringing. The financial math is essentially inverted — and yet foster-care adoption remains the path fewer prospective parents pursue, largely because of perceived complexity, trauma history, and uncertainty in the placement process.
