The closing argument

Five sections of data point toward one underused lever.

Sections 01–05 measured a system under strain. 1.124 million abortions per year. 167,000 children entering foster care. ~$34 billion already spent on a system performing at half-capacity. The argument over what abortion should be has consumed fifty years of American politics. The data tells a different story: under every plausible policy direction — stricter, looser, or unchanged — one upstream intervention reduces the strain on every downstream system at the same time.

That intervention is universal access to proactive, effective contraception. Not as a moral position, but as a measurable lever. The closest thing the United States has to a controlled experiment on this question already happened — in Colorado, between 2009 and 2019, with rigorously documented results. This section walks through what those results were, what they would look like at national scale, and what they would cost.

Six things the data shows

Foster alumni meet clinical PTSD criteria — roughly twice the rate of Iraq War veterans.
Children entering the foster system carry trauma not just from what brought them in, but from the placement instability and disruption the system itself produces. 1 in 5 alumni reach adulthood with diagnosable PTSD.
Source: Casey Family Programs, 2005 (n=1,087 alumni); Iraq comparison from Hoge et al., NEJM 2004
Anchor 03 · Sec. 03
$37K / 22 cases
A foster care caseworker earns $37,000 to manage 22 children — double the recommended caseload.
Each child carries roughly ~100 potential placement and permanency outcomes. The system was not designed to be evaluated by adults who can hold that much complexity at that scale, for that pay. Turnover has reached ~25% annually.
Source: BLS, 2024 (entry-level wages); CWLA Standards (recommended 12–15 ratio)
Anchor 04 · Sec. 04
25% → 65%
Proactive contraception use rises with income — access, not preference, drives the gap.
9 out of 10 women have tried some form of contraception. But the share using effective, proactive methods climbs from 25% among women below the poverty line to 65% among women above 200% of poverty — tracking the income line, not the moral one.
Source: Guttmacher Institute, Contraceptive Use in the U.S.
Anchor 05 · Sec. 05
$52–$62B
The foster system's projected budget by FY 2036, if it operates at published standards.
Forecasting forward from Section 05: meeting CWLA staffing ratios, FFPSA prevention targets, and Casey trauma-services recommendations against the +11% intake projection produces a $52B center, $62B high-end figure. The current spend is $34B.
Source: Section 05 forecast; Child Trends; JAMA Pediatrics, 2023
Anchor 06 · The natural experiment
50% ↓
Colorado's free-LARC program cut the teen abortion rate in half — and saved $5.85 per $1 spent.
Between 2009 and 2019, Colorado funded universal access to long-acting reversible contraception through Title X clinics. Teen birth and abortion rates fell ~50%; adult abortion rates fell ~20%. Every $1 in program spending returned $5.85 in avoided Medicaid birth costs alone.
Source: Colorado Department of Public Health and Environment; American Journal of Public Health, 2022

Pulling the threads together

The pattern underneath

The same population shows up in every dataset.

Read the six anchors together. 75% of women seeking abortion are below 200% of the federal poverty line. The same income tier shows the lowest proactive contraception use (25%). The same demographic supplies most foster care intake when economic strain triggers housing-inadequacy removals. The same alumni population shows up later in PTSD statistics, incarceration rates, and unemployment data.

This isn't a moral problem. It's a distribution problem. The interventions women above the poverty line use every day — IUDs, implants, and other long-acting methods — are the same interventions that don't reach the population most likely to feed every downstream system. Colorado closed that gap inside one state, with one funding source, over ten years. The result was measurable: fewer abortions, fewer unintended births, lower public spending, and higher graduation rates.

The closing question of this piece is whether the same intervention, scaled nationally, produces the same effect.

The Colorado Family Planning Initiative funded 69 Title X clinics to provide IUDs and implants at no cost to low-income women. Over ten years it served roughly 45,000 women. By 2019, the adolescent abortion rate had fallen from 39.6 per 1,000 to 13.5 per 1,000 — the steepest decline of any state. Independent analyses confirmed the program caused the decline rather than tracking a national trend: counties within 7 miles of a clinic saw the effects; further counties did not. The program also raised the high school graduation rate by 4 percentage points.

The argument

Common ground

A policy that returns $4 for every $1 spent should clear every test — fiscal, moral, practical.

The data in Sections 01–05 does not require agreement on when life begins. It does not require agreement on the proper scope of state authority over reproduction. It does not require agreement on the moral status of abortion itself.

It requires agreement on one observation: women below the poverty line use proactive contraception at one-third the rate of women above 200% of poverty — not because they want to, but because they can't easily access it. Close that gap and the data points toward measurable consequences in every direction. Fewer abortions. Fewer foster care entries. Lower public spending. Higher graduation rates. A foster system that can actually function within its budget. Children who don't reach adulthood with the trauma profile of combat veterans.

This is the only intervention measured in this piece that improves every metric simultaneously, costs less than it saves, and doesn't require resolving the abortion debate to be worth doing. The data does not tell anyone what to believe about abortion. It tells everyone what to do about the system we have.
