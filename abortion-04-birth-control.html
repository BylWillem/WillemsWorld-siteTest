A note on the headline number
The 41.3% figure is a snapshot of any given month, not lifetime use. 99% of sexually experienced women have used some form of contraception at some point in their lives. The remaining 58.7% includes women using less effective methods (condoms, withdrawal, fertility awareness) and those not currently using anything — most of whom are pregnant, postpartum, trying to conceive, or not sexually active. The number of women who are sexually active, want to avoid pregnancy, and are not using any method is roughly 1 in 6.

Sources: CDC NCHS Data Brief No. 539, August 2025 — National Survey of Family Growth (NSFG) 2022–2023, n=5,586 females; Planned Parenthood Action Fund — 99% lifetime use figure; KFF, Contraception in the United States (Sept 2025) — 17% of sexually active non-pregnant women not using contraception.

What this reveals
Women across every income level use contraception at remarkably similar rates — 87% at the bottom, 91% at the top. The gap isn't whether women try to prevent pregnancy. The gap is in effectiveness: low-income women rely more on pills, condoms, and female sterilization; higher-income women have IUDs, implants, and partner vasectomies. 40% of low-income women say they would use a different method if cost weren't a factor.

The two surprises
Female sterilization is more common than the pill. Most people assume the pill is the most-used method — it's not. 11.5% of women 15–49 are surgically sterilized, compared to 11.4% on the pill. And vasectomy is used by 5.6% of partners — more than fertility awareness, withdrawal, and emergency contraception combined.

LARC vs. the pill

A head-to-head on the two most popular proactive methods
Why the choice between them is the choice that matters most

The pill and LARC (IUD + implant) are both classified as proactive & effective — and they're used by roughly equal shares of American women (11.4% and 10.5% respectively). But they're not equivalent. The choice between them comes down to three trade-offs.

Side effects
What women actually experience
Systemic
Hormones travel through the whole body. Common: nausea, headaches, mood changes, decreased libido, breast tenderness, breakthrough bleeding. Serious risk: elevated blood clot risk — especially with smoking or after age 35. Daily compliance creates room for human error.
Localized
Hormonal IUDs release hormones locally; copper IUDs use no hormones at all. Adjustment phase: insertion pain, cramping, irregular bleeding for 3–6 months. Hormonal IUDs often eliminate periods; copper IUDs can make them heavier. Rare risks: uterine perforation (~1 in 1,000) or expulsion.
Average weight gain
From Cochrane reviews and meta-analyses
~0–2 kgfirst year
Combined pills: Cochrane review of 49 trials found no meaningful weight gain — weight-neutral on average. Perceived gains are mostly fluid retention (estrogen-driven), not fat.

Progestin-only pills: ~2 kg (4.4 lb) over 6–12 months in some studies. Mixed evidence.
0–2 kgin most studies
Hormonal IUD: Cochrane found limited evidence of weight change — less than 2 kg in most studies, similar to non-hormonal groups.

Copper IUD: Hormone-free, no documented weight effect.

RR 1.40hormonal IUD
Hormonal IUD (LNG): RR 1.40 — 40% higher risk — notably higher than the combined pill, despite local hormone delivery.
Patch/ring: RR 1.6–2.0 — 60–100% higher.
Copper IUD: Hormone-free. No documented mood effect.

The local-delivery assumption underestimates systemic effects. Adolescents using non-oral hormonal methods had RR > 3 (three times the risk).
Access & effort
What it takes to use
Continuous
Daily pill at the same time. Monthly refills. Annual prescription renewal. One pill (Opill) is now OTC as of 2023 — the rest require a prescription. Missing a single dose can break the cycle and reduce effectiveness.
One-and-done
Single outpatient insertion appointment, often with a preceding consult. Then nothing for 3–12 years. Removal requires another appointment. Provider availability is uneven — not all OB-GYNs insert IUDs, and some require multiple visits before placement.
What the comparison reveals
On every dimension, LARC is more effective, more durable, and cheaper over time. But it requires a single, expensive moment — a procedure with upfront cost (insured or uninsured) and an OB-GYN appointment that not every provider performs. The pill is the opposite: lower friction at each step, but lower effectiveness and higher cost over time. This is exactly why the income gap exists. Women without the resources to navigate that single expensive moment land on the pill instead — even when an IUD would serve them better.

The cost barrier compounds with income. 40% of low-income women not currently using contraception say they would start using a method if cost weren't a barrier. 23% of low-income current users say they would switch to a more effective method if cost weren't an issue. The pattern from subsection 2 — that women across incomes use contraception at similar rates but with different effectiveness — is the direct downstream effect of these access gaps.
The two-part problem
The data points to a side-effect problem and an access problem happening simultaneously. Side effects (65% cite concerns, 31% currently experiencing) suggest the methods themselves still have real costs to women's bodies and lives that the system underacknowledges. But the access problem is more solvable: cost, information, and insurance friction account for nearly half of why women don't use their preferred method. A system that closed the access gap wouldn't solve everything — but it would close half of the gap, and most of the half that's tractable.
