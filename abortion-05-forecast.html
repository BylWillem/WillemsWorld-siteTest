In 2024, U.S. clinicians provided 1,124,000 abortions — an increase of 21% from 2020, the last full year before Dobbs. The states that restricted abortion didn't reduce the national total; they shifted where abortions take place. The redistribution effect
The bars don't line up. 28% of women of reproductive age live in total-ban states — but those states host 0% of the formal abortions. The 28-point gap doesn't vanish; it gets absorbed mostly by viability-limit and no-limit states, plus cross-state travel. In 2024, ~155,000 people crossed state lines for abortion care, and shield-law providers in protected states sent an estimated 34,500 medication abortions by mail into ban states in the first half of 2024 alone. Self-managed abortions outside the clinical system aren't counted in either bar.

The four absorber states
Four states became the de facto destinations for displaced abortion demand — absorbing the bulk of cross-state travel in 2024: Illinois (35,000 out-of-state patients, 39% of state total), North Carolina (16,700, 36% of state total), Kansas (16,100, 71% of state total), and New Mexico (12,800, 69% of state total). Together they absorbed roughly ~80,000 of the ~155,000 cross-state travelers. North Carolina is the only state where this story is mixed — it absorbs significant out-of-state demand and shows an overall decline because its 12-week ban and 72-hour waiting period limit access for its own residents. Map color reflects total in-state change; the absorber rank reflects travel destination.

How to read this
The smallest effect (foster intake, +11%) takes the longest to appear and is the most disputed; the largest effects (sterilization spikes, +100–200%) happened within months. Each of these is a measured change — not a model output, not a projection — from published peer-reviewed and government data. The four dimension cards below each unpack one band: what changed, by how much, where, and why.

The y-axis is non-linear to fit effect sizes that range from +11% to +200% on a single chart. Band widths are illustrative of state-to-state and study-to-study variation; they are not formal confidence intervals. The four metrics are independent indicators of system stress, not a composite index. Each band's endpoint reflects the largest defensible measured change in the relevant cohort and time range. Underlying sources are detailed in each dimension card below.

The supporting evidence
  • JAMA Pediatrics (2023): Beth Israel Deaconess / Bentley study of 4M+ foster placements from 2000–2020. First-trimester pregnancy in a state with a TRAP law was associated with an 11% increase in foster care entries.
  • Mechanism: The increase was attributable primarily to housing inadequacy as the reason for removal — not abuse or neglect. Restricting abortion didn't worsen parenting; it worsened the financial conditions parents were already struggling with.
  • Demographic overlap: 75% of women seeking abortion have incomes below 200% of the federal poverty level. The population affected by restriction overlaps directly with the population most likely to enter the child welfare system.
  • Caveat: The full downstream effect won't be measurable for years — foster care entries typically follow conception by 2–6 years. The 11% figure is from the TRAP-law era; Dobbs-era impacts will appear in foster intake data through ~2028.

Sources: Wallace, Vlasak & Strane, “Association Between Restricted Abortion Access and Child Entries Into the Foster Care System,” JAMA Pediatrics (2023) — n=4M+ placements 2000–2020, 11% effect attributable to housing inadequacy; Harvard Medical School coverage; AFCARS Foster Care Statistics Dashboard — FY 2020–2024 snapshot counts (407K, 391K, 369K, 343K, 329K); Milbank Memorial Fund, “The Impact of Restrictive State Abortion Laws” (2025) — 22K additional births in banned states. Foster care entries · FY 2005–FY 2024 observed, FY 2025–FY 2036 forecast
The COVID gap, and the slow return

Two patterns shape this chart. First, a real structural decline in foster intake from 2016 onward, driven by the Family First Prevention Services Act and shifting state policy. Second, a sharp drop in 2020–2024 that is partly real and partly a measurement artifact — pandemic-era school closures, court backlogs, and caseworker turnover made the system temporarily blind to cases it would normally have caught. Layered on top of both: the slow-moving effect of post-Dobbs restriction, which won't fully appear in the data until the late 2020s.

Why COVID broke the data

FY 2020–2022 entries dropped not because fewer children needed help, but because the system temporarily lost the ability to find them. Four mechanisms compounded:

  • Mandatory reporters lost contact. Most child abuse reports come from teachers, doctors, daycare workers, and other professionals who see kids regularly. School closures and the shift to telehealth removed those reporting channels for over a year.
  • Investigations slowed. Child welfare agencies couldn't physically enter homes the way they had before. In-person visits were replaced by Zoom and phone calls, sharply limiting what investigators could observe and act on.
  • Court systems backlogged. Foster care removals require court approval. Pandemic-era court closures delayed hearings by months, which delayed the formal "entry" event — some children weren't processed until well into FY 2021.
  • Caseworker turnover peaked at ~25% in 2021 (up from a pre-pandemic baseline of 10–15%), with nearly half of newly hired caseworkers leaving within 12 months. Job stress, burnout, and remote-work transitions hollowed out agencies' capacity to process cases that did get reported.
Why the forecast ramps gradually instead of all at once

A policy change in June 2022 doesn't show up in foster intake data the same year. The lag from conception to foster care entry is typically 2–6 years — long enough for a child to be born, reach toddlerhood, and have the economic strain that causes "housing inadequacy" removals fully surface in a household. The JAMA Pediatrics study (the source of the +11% effect) measured this lag explicitly: their analysis correlated first-trimester pregnancies under TRAP laws with foster entries several years later. So the post-Dobbs effect appears slowly — barely visible in FY 2024, beginning to crest around FY 2026–2028, and not fully realized until the early 2030s. The chart's ±15% confidence band reflects the genuine uncertainty in how much of the underlying decline (driven by the Family First Prevention Services Act and kinship-care reforms) will continue to counterbalance the upward pressure from restriction.

Annual child welfare spending · current vs. standards-meeting
The dollars the system actually requires

In SFY 2022, child welfare agencies across all 50 states spent $34.3 billion. That money funds caseworkers, foster homes, adoption assistance, investigations, and prevention services for the ~370,000 children in care on any given day. The question this card asks is simple: how much would it cost to operate the system at the standards the field's own professional bodies already publish? Not advocacy targets — just published recommendations from the Child Welfare League of America, the federal Children's Bureau, and Casey Family Programs.

Where the additional $12 billion would go
  • Caseworker headcount (+$6.3B): Current ratios are 24–31 cases per worker; CWLA standards recommend 12–15. Doubling caseworker capacity nearly doubles administration spending from $7.5B to $13.8B.
  • Prevention & family preservation (+$3.8B): Currently 5% of the budget at $1.7B; the FFPSA-era target and CWLA position is 12–15%. Expanding evidence-based prevention services to $5.5B could reduce downstream foster intake by an estimated 10–20%.
  • Mental health & trauma services (+$3.7B): 54% of foster alumni have a clinical-level mental health problem (Casey study); a fraction receive ongoing care. A dedicated $3.7B trauma-services category doesn't exist today — the spending is scattered across Medicaid and "other."
  • Foster placement & payments (+$1.8B): Total dollars grow from $12.9B to $14.7B — partly because of more children entering care (the +11% intake forecast), partly because average monthly stipends of $400–$800 are estimated at roughly half the actual cost of raising a child with foster-system needs.
  • Aging-out support (+$0.9B): Roughly 20,000 youth age out annually with little structured support. The Chafee program funds $143 million nationally — about $7,000 per youth. Bringing this in line with kinship-guardianship support raises this line to $1.4B.

Adoption assistance and the "other" category stay roughly flat in absolute dollars; some current in-home-services spending is recategorized into prevention. Increases sum to about $16.5B in new spending; recategorization offsets and efficiency savings net the total back to roughly +$12B.

Why the budget also needs to grow — not just rebalance

Today's $46B figure assumes today's caseload. The intake forecast from the previous chart projects FY 2036 entries at 295K (center) with a ±15% confidence band of 251K–339K. Scaling the standards-meeting budget proportionally produces a range of $52B (center) to $62B (high end). Most of that growth flows through the foster placement and mental health categories, which scale roughly linearly with the number of children in care. Categories like prevention and aging-out support are largely fixed costs once funded at standard.
